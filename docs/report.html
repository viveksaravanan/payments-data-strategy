Payments Data Strategy: Cross-Merchant Intelligence Through Privacy-Preserved Analytics

Executive summary

A payments company sees a join no one else does — the basket, the payment instrument, and the same physical customer across every merchant they shop. This prototype turns that vantage point into a working system: capture → anonymize → store → insight, end to end, with two complementary AI agents on top.

Three fictional merchants — Kroger, Taco Bell, TJ Maxx — share a 5,000-customer synthetic panel for 90 days. The pipeline produces two parallel data layers: a tenant layer with each merchant's own granular transactions, and a lake layer that implements the dual-path privacy architecture with PAN tokenization, ZIP3 + hour-bucket generalization, and k=5 anonymity (three of the six §8.2 techniques; see the anonymization section for what's deferred). Sitting on top: a Merchant Advisor that answers ops questions for a single merchant by combining its tenant data with peer benchmarks from the lake, and a Network Analyst that surfaces cross-merchant patterns no single merchant could see alone.

Why a payments network can do this

Three parties in the commerce stack each see a slice of every transaction. Only one sees enough to answer cross-merchant questions about real human shopping behavior.

Card networks

Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Discover

  • × The basket (no SKU detail)
  • ✓ The payment instrument
  • ✓ Same person across merchants — but only via the card

POS / loyalty vendors

Toast, NCR, in-store loyalty programs

  • ✓ The basket at full SKU detail
  • × The payment instrument (mostly)
  • × The same person at other merchants

Payments network — you

Acquirer / processor with merchant data partnerships

  • ✓ The basket (via merchant data)
  • ✓ The payment instrument
  • ✓ Same person across all merchants in the network

The third column is the strategic argument. A card network has the join key but not the basket; a POS vendor has the basket but not the join key. A payments network sitting on top of merchant data has both — and that combination is what the lake layer in this prototype is built to expose, safely.

The three-merchant panel

The panel deliberately spans three different segments — grocery, QSR, and off-price retail — because different segments produce different basket shapes, transaction cadences, and payment mixes. Different shapes are exactly what makes cross-merchant comparisons interesting; an analysis where everyone behaves the same has nothing to find.

How the data was generated

Synthetic data only earns its keep when it has the same statistical texture as production data. The generator captures the rhythms, behavioral clusters, and edge cases the agents need something to find.

The shared customer panel and the cross-merchant invariant

5,000 customers are generated once with full PII (name, email, ZIP5) and a stable 16-digit synthetic PAN. Each merchant generator picks a participation share (Kroger 90%, Taco Bell 60%, TJ Maxx 40%) from this single panel. The same physical customer always carries the same PAN to every merchant they shop. After hashing, that single PAN becomes a single customer_id in the lake — the foundation of every cross-merchant query later.

Behavioral segments and bimodal baskets

About 70% of customers behave as "fillers" — small frequent baskets — and 30% as "stockers" — large biweekly baskets. Within a single shopping trip, basket size is drawn from a bimodal mixture so the data has both quick-trip and weekly-haul shapes co-existing realistically. About 5% of customers are flagged as "lapsers" who transact only once or twice in the 90-day window, putting a long tail on retention questions.

Day-of-week and pay-cycle patterns

Each merchant has segment-appropriate peak days (Sat–Sun for grocery and retail, Fri–Sat for QSR) and peak hours (evenings + weekend mornings for grocery, lunch + dinner for QSR, weekend afternoons for retail). On top of that, a ~15% pay-cycle bump on the 1st–3rd and 15th–17th of every month captures paychecks and SNAP issuance. Affinity pairs (what gets bought together)

Without deliberate co-purchase patterns, basket-affinity questions return noise. A small number of anchor → companion rules are wired into the basket sampler. They're a small fraction of all line items, but they're enough that "what gets bought with milk?" returns a sensible list rather than random SKUs.

Three planted anomalies

To give the agent something genuinely interesting to find, three signals are deliberately injected at Kroger:

  1. Price spike. SKU KRG-PRODUCE-0042 (avocados) priced at 5× baseline on a single day in the last 7 days — a glitch the operations team would want to catch.
  2. Store dropout. Store KRG-OH-0011 sees ~30% fewer transactions in the last 7 days vs. the prior week — the kind of soft signal a regional manager wants surfaced before it becomes a quarterly miss.
  3. Cohort surge. ~50 customers who never bought baby SKUs before suddenly start in the last 21 days — the "new parent" cohort, the most strategically valuable customer transition a grocer can spot.

Two of these anomalies are visible in the daily-volume chart above — if you know where to look. The agents find them by writing SQL.

The dual-table-prefix database design

SQLite holds two parallel sets of tables sharing one merchants dimension. The tenant_* prefix is what each merchant sees about itself: hashed customer IDs but full granularity on everything else. The lake_* prefix is what the network sees across merchants: same hashed IDs, but ZIP3 instead of ZIP5, hour-bucketed timestamps, k-anonymity-suppressed cells, and category-level baskets instead of SKU-level. Same database file, two consistent views.

Anonymization: the dual-path pipeline

⚠

Note: this is a partial implementation. Strategy doc §8.2 specifies six anonymization techniques; this demo builds three of them — PAN tokenization, ZIP3 + hour-bucket generalization, and k-anonymity. The k threshold is set to k = 5 rather than the spec's k ≥ 50 because the demo dataset (5,000 customers) is too small for k = 50 to leave usable data; the architecture supports any k.

Not yet implemented: transaction amount binning, l-diversity within k-anonymous groups, ε-bounded differential privacy on aggregates, and low-volume entity suppression. Each is incremental work on the existing architecture rather than a rewrite — see ARCHITECTURE.md for the roadmap.

This is the strategically critical part. Two stages, two outputs, two purposes — and the only thing that lets a payments network do cross-merchant analytics without ever exposing a real human.

Stage 1 — Tenant processing

  • Drop PII columns. customer_name and customer_email are removed entirely — they never appear in tenant output.
  • Hash the PAN. customer_id = sha256(secret + pan)[:16]. Irreversible. Deterministic — the same PAN at any merchant produces the same 16-character hex string.
  • Keep everything else. Full timestamps, full ZIP5, full basket detail, real prices. Tenant data is the merchant's own — granularity is preserved for their analytics.

Stage 2 — Lake processing

  • Truncate ZIPs. home_zip5 → home_zip3; store_zip5 → store_zip3. ZIP3 covers ~100,000 people on average — too broad to identify individuals.
  • Hour-bucket the timestamps. A new txn_hour_bucket column rounds every transaction down to the start of its hour, alongside the original timestamp.
  • Collapse SKU detail to category. Lake transaction items are aggregated to (txn_id, category) — the lake doesn't need SKU-level snooping, and merchants get to keep their SKU-level patterns proprietary.
  • k=5 anonymity. Group customers by (age_band, income_band, home_zip3); any group with fewer than 5 customers has its home_zip3 set to NULL.

customers had their home_zip3 suppressed by k=5 on this run — meaning the cell that would have made them re-identifiable was set to NULL. The architecture supports any k; a production deployment per Strategy Doc §8.2 would use k=50.

And here's why all this work matters

The hash is deterministic, so the same physical customer's customer_id is identical across all three merchants. That single fact is what makes cross-merchant joins possible in the lake — without ever holding a name, an email, or a usable card number anywhere in the database.

The AI agents

Strategy Doc §10.2 enumerates seven AI agents. This prototype builds two — the Conversational Business Advisor in two flavors, one tenant-aware (Merchant Advisor) and one network-only (Network Analyst). The other five are noted as roadmap.

Tool surface: who can see what

The Merchant Advisor has access to its merchant's tenant data and the cross-merchant lake. The Network Analyst has access to the lake only — no tenant-table tool exists in its tool list, so the agent literally cannot query a single merchant's proprietary data even if asked. This is a real isolation barrier, not a polite request.

Solid lines: tools the agent can call. Dashed line with ×: query_tenant is not in the analyst's tool list at the API layer — the model literally cannot emit a call to it.

What's built · What's deferred

Honest accounting. The prototype is a vertical slice, not the production system.

Built today

  • Three-merchant synthetic data generator with realistic basket / day / hour / pay-cycle / affinity behavior
  • Two-stage anonymization (tenant + lake) with deterministic SHA-256 hashing, ZIP3 truncation, hour bucketing, k=5 anonymity
  • SQLite database, dual-table-prefix design, foreign-keyed, indexed
  • Two AI agents (Merchant Advisor, Network Analyst) with tool-level isolation and a SELECT-only SQL guard
  • Streamlit dashboard with role selector, canned questions, free-text input, MOCK MODE for offline demos
  • 69 passing tests covering generation, anonymization, DB integrity, and agent guard rails

Deferred to roadmap

  • The other 5 agents (Demand Forecasting, Dynamic Pricing, Location Intelligence, Payment Optimization, standalone Anomaly Detection) Strategy §10.2
  • k=50 anonymity (current k=5 documented in ARCHITECTURE.md) Strategy §8.2
  • l-diversity and differential-privacy noise (DP stub only) Strategy §8.2
  • Real-time pipeline (Kafka, Flink, sub-second latency) — current pipeline is batch Strategy §9
  • Real authn/authz for tenant isolation (currently enforced by SQL filtering, not access control) Strategy §8.3
  • Hardware/edge story (terminal simulator, P2PE ceremony, mTLS to cloud) Strategy §3–6
  • Annual seasonality (90-day window doesn't cover it)
  • Postgres or any DB more complex than SQLite

The argument, restated

A payments company's strategic data asset isn't the payment metadata, and isn't the basket either. It's the join between them, anchored on an identity that's stable across merchants. Card networks can't write the basket query. POS vendors can't write the cross-merchant query. A payments network sitting on top of merchant data can write both — and the dual-path architecture demonstrated here is what lets it do so safely, without holding any data that could re-identify a real human.

This prototype is the floor of that vision, not the ceiling. The architectural shape — tenant tables for granular merchant analytics, lake tables for anonymized cross-merchant analytics, AI agents with role-appropriate tool access — is what scales to k=50, to billions of transactions per day, to seven agents, to real authn/authz, and to all the rest of the strategy doc. What's here is enough to make the argument concretely: 769 customers, $880 + $521 + $64, in <15 seconds, from a database that holds no PII.

Synthetic data only. No real customers, real cards, or real merchants are involved. The merchant names are plausible-sounding stand-ins for grocery / QSR / off-price retail; this prototype is not affiliated with Kroger, Taco Bell, or TJ Maxx.

Privacy implementation is partial — see the Anonymization section for details.
