Payments Data Strategy

Unlocking the value of transaction data across merchants.

A working prototype of cross-merchant payments intelligence: each merchant can compare itself to others, while never seeing another merchant's raw data. This page explains how a system like this can be built.

The thesis: a payment terminal sits in the middle of every checkout — reading the basket, the payment, and the merchant context. Across an installed base of merchants, that one vantage point becomes cross-merchant intelligence no single merchant can see on its own.

How customers shop

Six mechanisms turn the shoppers' hidden traits into transactions.

Gravity — store choice follows a gravity rule: bigger, nearer stores pull harder, and the pull drops off with distance.
Affinity — some things get bought together: pasta → sauce runs 2.39× more likely than chance.
Daypart & timing — grocery skews to weekends (1.25× a weekday); Taco Bell runs late, Burger King leans breakfast, Chick-fil-A is closed Sundays.
Seasonality — across the window, spending drifts up as spring warms and Easter week lifts baking, ham, and candy — the spikes land in the right categories, not across the board.
Private label — where a shelf offers both, value shoppers lean store-brand and affluent shoppers lean name-brand. Store-brand unit share: Kroger 27.6%, Winn-Dixie 26.3%, Acme 19.3%.
Missions — every trip has a reason, and the reason sets the basket. The top 20% of grocery baskets carry 41.9% of all units.
How this works

A weekly stock-up fills a big basket; a quick trip grabs three things. The long right tail is the stock-up behavior every grocer sees.

The window runs early March to late May. The lift concentrates in Easter categories rather than spreading evenly across the basket.

SECTION 2

Comparing to your peers without seeing their data

The point of the system is to let a merchant compare itself to its competitors. The rule it must never break is that no merchant sees another's raw data. It holds both by keeping two data paths completely separate.

A merchant sees its own data in full detail: every store, product, and transaction, with the real identifiers. It sees its peers' data only through a separate, anonymized peer lake. The two never mix. Raw competitor rows are never copied into the merchant's view, so there is nothing there for a query to reach.

Two kinds of protection do the work. Four are baked into the lake when it is built, so the sensitive data isn't there to leak. Two more are checked every time a question is asked.

The privacy mechanisms

Built into the databefore any question
You're excluded from your own view. Your own rows are dropped when your copy of the lake is built — not filtered out at read time, so no query can recover them.
No competitor is named. Each peer shows up only as peer or merchant; the real banner code is stripped, with no stable nickname to follow one competitor across rows.
IDs are one-way; time becomes a band. IDs turn into SHA-256 tokens you can't reverse. The clock is dropped to one of ten time-of-day bands (hour×10÷24) plus the date — "late morning," not 10:47am.
Shared categories, neighborhood only, no shopper. Only the categories that match across merchants; the neighborhood, never an address; and no customer id of any kind.
Checked at query timeevery question
Aggregates only. A peer query can return grouped sums, averages, and counts — never a single line. The rule is checked on the parsed query, so how the question is phrased can't get around it.
The k = 50 floor. Any result cell built on fewer than 50 underlying purchase lines is dropped before the agent sees it, and the number held back comes back as suppressed.

A note on posture. This runs on synthetic data, so k = 50 is the live floor here and there is no real merchant behind the numbers. On real data, a production deployment would go further still: adding statistical noise to published aggregates (differential privacy) and requiring a minimum variety within each released cell (l-diversity), on top of the k = 50 floor.

SECTION 3

The agents: every number is checked before you see it

A question in plain English becomes a grounded answer. The system routes the question, runs real queries, and validates every figure against the data before displaying it.

A free-form question is classified by a lightweight router to the specialist best suited to answer it; a clicked suggested question skips the router and goes straight to its specialist. Anything that doesn't fit a specialist falls to a conversational advisor.

What each agent is for

  • Pricing — how your prices compare to peers, category by category, in real dollars, and where you have room to move.
  • Demand — category and basket performance, and where you over- or under-perform your peer set.
  • Trade area — neighborhood and store-level performance, and expansion opportunity.
  • Anomaly — unusual spikes and drops, including the planted University City decline, set against peer baselines.
  • Conversational advisor — free-form questions, and anything that spans more than one specialist.

Peer comparison is only offered when it's honest. The three grocers each have two same-segment peers; Taco Bell and TJ Maxx have none in this panel, so a pricing-style peer comparison correctly declines for them rather than inventing one — while trend and trade-area views still work on their own data.

The trust guarantee. The agent reasons over real query results, not its own memory. Before any answer is shown, every number in it is checked against an actual cell in the query result — within tolerance — and any figure that can't be traced is removed, not displayed. The model cannot surface a fabricated number. This grounding check is the credibility core of the whole system. (Today an answer is prose, grounded numbers, and a result table; charts inside answers are a planned fast-follow.)
