Payments Data Strategy · v4 Demonstrator

A merchant network sees a join no one else can — and this shows how to use it safely.

A working prototype of cross-merchant payments intelligence: each merchant can compare itself to its competitors on real, like-for-like numbers, while never seeing another merchant's raw data. This page explains how the system is built, and the key assumptions behind it.

All data is synthetic. The five merchants are plausible stand-ins generated from scratch — no real customers, cards, or companies are involved. The point is to demonstrate the architecture, not to report on any real business.

SECTION 1

The data: built to be realistic, not convenient

Everything in the demo runs on synthetic data. The credibility of the whole system rests on one question — is the simulated data realistic enough to trust? Here is how it was built, and why.

The panel is five real-brand stand-ins across three genuinely different retail segments, all in a single metro modeled on Charlotte, North Carolina:

  • Grocery — Kroger, Acme, and Winn-Dixie (5 stores each).
  • Quick-service restaurant — Taco Bell (9 stores).
  • Off-price retail — TJ Maxx (5 stores).

Across 90 days, 100,000 cards generate 1.66 million transactions and 10.76 million line items. The distinction matters: a transaction is one visit; a line item is one product within it. Real peer comparisons (basket size, category mix, unit pricing) live at the line-item level, so the data is generated and stored at that grain.

The scale is deliberate. A small dataset can't support stable aggregate patterns or statistically meaningful peer comparisons — with 100,000 cards and ten million lines, a "your dairy price vs. the peer average" comparison rests on thousands of observations, not a handful.

The assumptions that make it realistic

The data isn't random noise. A sequence of deliberate generation decisions gives it the structure a real network would have:

  • Merchants aren't clones. Each merchant is generated with its own pricing, volume, and mix biases — premium (Acme, ~$58 average basket), mainstream (Kroger, ~$52), and value (Winn-Dixie, ~$50). That spread is what makes a peer comparison non-trivial instead of everyone landing on the same number.
  • Segments behave like their real-world counterparts. Grocery is frequent visits with large, mixed baskets; QSR is high-frequency, small, simple tickets; off-price is infrequent and variable. The chart below shows the three segments separating cleanly on exactly these axes.
  • A realistic payment mix. 54% credit / 46% debit; contactless 53.5%, chip 36.4%, swipe 8.7%; 16.7% of taps via mobile wallet; card networks led by Visa (55%) and Mastercard (31%) — set to plausible present-day shares.
  • Plausible geography and timing. Stores sit in eight real Charlotte neighborhoods with coordinates; purchase volume follows day-of-week and time-of-day rhythms.
  • Reproducible by construction. Generation flows from a single fixed seed through eight causal layers (geography → population → customers → trips → baskets → payment → pricing → events). The same seed produces byte-for-byte identical data every run.
  • Planted, discoverable patterns. Real signals are deliberately seeded so the analytics have something true to surface — most notably a multi-week traffic decline in the University City and Eastway neighborhoods (hardest on Winn-Dixie), shown below.
  • Privacy designed in from the start. Sensitive attributes (a customer's affluence and loyalty profile) are generated to drive behavior but are structurally prevented from ever reaching the peer-sharing layer — covered in Section 2.
SECTION 2

Sharing safely: two separate paths for your data and your peers'

The value is cross-merchant comparison; the constraint is that no merchant may see another's raw data. The architecture solves this with two strictly separated paths.

A merchant sees its own data in full, identifiable detail — every store, product, and transaction. It sees its peers' data only through a separate, anonymized peer lake. The two never co-mingle: raw competitor rows are physically incapable of reaching the merchant or its agents.

The four privacy mechanisms

  • Identity is hidden. A peer is labeled only peer (same segment as you) or merchant (a different segment) — never a name or even a stable pseudonym.
  • k = 5 suppression at query time. Any result group backed by fewer than five underlying records is dropped before the agent sees it, and the agent is told that coverage was thin rather than shown a sparse number.
  • Aggregates only. A query against the peer lake can only return sums, averages, and counts over groups — never an individual transaction line. This is enforced on the parsed SQL itself, not by trust.
  • You are absent from your own peer view. Each merchant gets its own copy of the lake with its rows structurally removed, so a merchant can never recover itself from "the peers."

An honest note on posture. This is a deliberately minimal privacy posture for a demonstrator running on synthetic data, where there is no real merchant to re-identify. A production deployment would raise the suppression floor (k ≥ 50) and add differential-privacy noise to published aggregates. We state this plainly — the demo shows the architecture; the stronger guarantees are a configuration change, not a redesign.

SECTION 3

The agents: every number is checked before you see it

A question in plain English becomes a grounded answer. The system routes the question, runs real queries, and validates every figure against the data before displaying it.

A free-form question is classified by a lightweight router to the specialist best suited to answer it; a clicked suggested question skips the router and goes straight to its specialist. Anything that doesn't fit a specialist falls to a conversational advisor.

What each agent is for

  • Pricing — how your prices compare to peers, category by category, in real dollars, and where you have room to move.
  • Demand — category and basket performance, and where you over- or under-perform your peer set.
  • Trade area — neighborhood and store-level performance, and expansion opportunity.
  • Anomaly — unusual spikes and drops, including the planted University City decline, set against peer baselines.
  • Conversational advisor — free-form questions, and anything that spans more than one specialist.

Peer comparison is only offered when it's honest. The three grocers each have two same-segment peers; Taco Bell and TJ Maxx have none in this panel, so a pricing-style peer comparison correctly declines for them rather than inventing one — while trend and trade-area views still work on their own data.

The trust guarantee. The agent reasons over real query results, not its own memory. Before any answer is shown, every number in it is checked against an actual cell in the query result — within tolerance — and any figure that can't be traced is removed, not displayed. The model cannot surface a fabricated number. This grounding check is the credibility core of the whole system. (Today an answer is prose, grounded numbers, and a result table; charts inside answers are a planned fast-follow.)
